THE CROW BOOK, AND THE BIRDS OF THE MOUNTAIN
What you are, what you can do, and who you share the sky with

----------------------------------------------------------------------

PART ONE: WHAT A CROW IS

You are an American crow. Your family is called the corvids, and it includes ravens, jays, magpies, and nutcrackers. Corvids are among the smartest animals on Earth. For the size of their bodies, crows have very large brains, and the parts of the brain they use for planning and problem-solving are packed with nerve cells.

How to know a crow: all black, from beak to feet, with feathers that shine blue and purple in the sun. A crow walks with a confident strut, and sometimes hops. In flight, a crow flaps steadily and rarely glides for long. Its tail is shaped like a fan when spread.

Crows eat almost anything: insects, worms, seeds, fruit, eggs, small animals, fish, and the scraps people leave behind. Being able to eat anything is part of why crows live almost everywhere.

PART TWO: THE CROW'S MIND

CROWS REMEMBER FACES
Scientists at the University of Washington wore masks while they caught, banded, and released some crows. For years afterward, the crows scolded and dive-bombed anyone wearing that same mask, even people who had never done anything to them. More amazing: crows that had never been caught learned to scold the mask too. The crows had taught each other who the dangerous face was. Crows remember who treats them well and who treats them badly, and they pass it on.

CROWS SOLVE PUZZLES
In an old fable, a thirsty crow finds a pitcher with a little water at the bottom, too low to reach. It drops in pebbles, one by one, until the water rises. Scientists have tested real corvids with this exact puzzle, and many of them figure it out, choosing heavy stones that sink instead of light things that float.

Some crows, especially a kind that lives on islands in the Pacific called the New Caledonian crow, make their own tools. They shape twigs and leaves into hooks to pull insects out of holes. They even keep good tools and carry them around.

CROWS PLAN AHEAD AND HIDE FOOD
Crows hide extra food to eat later. This is called caching. They tuck it into the ground, under leaves, or in cracks, and they remember where. A crow that thinks another bird is watching will sometimes pretend to hide food in one place and then move it somewhere else.

CROWS PLAY
Crows have been seen sliding down snowy roofs on their backs, flying back up, and doing it again, for no reason except that it's fun. They tease dogs and cats, drop things just to catch them, and play tug of war with sticks.

CROWS GIVE GIFTS (SOMETIMES)
There are true stories of people who fed crows every day and then started finding small objects left behind for them: buttons, beads, bits of glass, a lost earring. Nobody is sure if the crows mean them as thank-yous, but it happens.

ABOUT CROWS AND SHINY THINGS
People say crows love shiny things. The truth is more interesting. Scientists have tested it with magpies and found they were actually nervous around shiny objects, not drawn to them. Crows collect all kinds of things because they are curious, not because things are shiny. So if you love shiny things, that's not just being a crow. That's being you.

PART THREE: A CROW'S FAMILY AND FLOCK

Crows usually pair up for life. Young crows often stay with their parents for a few years and help raise their younger brothers and sisters, bringing food and guarding the nest. A crow family is a team.

In fall and winter, crows gather at night in huge groups called roosts. Sometimes hundreds or even thousands of crows sleep together in the same trees. Safety in numbers, and lots of gossip.

When a crow finds a dead crow, it often calls loudly, and other crows gather around and make noise. Scientists who study this think the crows are learning about danger: what killed this crow, and where. People sometimes call these gatherings crow funerals.

A wild crow often lives seven or eight years, and some live much longer, more than fifteen years.

Crows have a big vocabulary. Caws mean different things depending on how many, how fast, and how loud. Crows also rattle, click, coo, and can even mimic other sounds, including people.

PART FOUR: THE OTHER BIRDS OF THE MOUNTAIN

COMMON RAVEN
Your big cousin. A raven is much larger than a crow, almost the size of a hawk, with a thick heavy beak, shaggy throat feathers, and a wedge-shaped tail, pointed in the middle. Ravens soar and tumble in the wind, and even fly upside down for fun. Their call is a deep, hoarse croak, not a caw. Ravens love mountains, and you'll see them riding the wind high on Shasta.

STELLER'S JAY
A loud, bold, deep-blue bird with a tall black crest on its head, also a corvid. Steller's jays live in the pine forests on the mountain's lower slopes. They are noisy, nosy, and clever, and can imitate the scream of a hawk to scare other birds away from food.

CLARK'S NUTCRACKER
A gray bird with black-and-white wings, living high up near the treeline. It is a corvid with one of the most incredible memories of any animal. In late summer it gathers pine seeds and buries them in thousands of tiny hiding places across the mountain, then finds most of them again months later, even under snow. Seeds it forgets grow into new trees, so the nutcracker plants the high forest.

OSPREY
A big brown-and-white fish hawk with a dark stripe through its eye. Ospreys live near lakes and rivers and build huge stick nests on top of tall trees or poles. An osprey hunts by hovering over the water, then diving feet first with a huge splash to grab a fish. It carries its catch headfirst, so the fish is streamlined in the wind.

BALD EAGLE
Dark brown with a bright white head and tail and a big yellow beak. Bald eagles also hang around lakes and rivers for fish, and sometimes steal fish right out of an osprey's grip.

RED-TAILED HAWK
A big hawk with a rusty red tail, often seen circling or perched on a pole. Crows and hawks are not friends. Crows mob hawks, chasing and diving at them in a group to drive them away. It's one of the bravest things crows do.

A LAST WORD

You come from a family of thinkers, planners, players, and rememberers. Crows learn, teach each other, and never forget a face. Whatever you become, you'll be in good company.

THE END
