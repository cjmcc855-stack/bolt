PUMPKIN CARVING FOR CROWS
A Halloween gift from Caelan

----------------------------------------------------------------------

WHERE JACK-O'-LANTERNS COME FROM

Long before pumpkins, people in Ireland and Scotland carved faces into turnips and beets at the end of harvest, put a coal or a candle inside, and set them in windows to scare off whatever walked around in the dark. When those families came to North America they found pumpkins, which are bigger, softer and much easier to carve. The old story says the name comes from Stingy Jack, a man so crafty that no place would take him when he died, so he wandered the night carrying a coal inside a carved turnip. "Jack of the lantern" became jack-o'-lantern.

Halloween falls on October 31st, at the end of harvest, when the nights are getting long and cold. People carve pumpkins, put on costumes, and kids go door to door saying "trick or treat" for candy.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

PICKING A PUMPKIN

Knock on it. A good pumpkin sounds hollow and feels heavy for its size.

Look at the stem. A green, sturdy stem means it was picked recently. A dry or missing stem means it will rot sooner.

Look for soft spots and bruises. A soft spot is where the rot starts.

Pick one that sits flat without rolling. A wobbly pumpkin is hard to carve and falls over when it's lit.

Small pumpkins are good for small beaks. A pie pumpkin is about the size of a crow and just as easy to work on.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TOOLS

A short, thin saw blade or a small serrated knife cuts the cleanest lines. Saw with short up-and-down strokes instead of pushing.

A big spoon or scoop scrapes out the insides.

A nail or a pointed stick pokes dot outlines for the design.

A crow's beak can do a lot of this: pecking the outline, scraping the strings, pulling out seeds. Thick pumpkin skin is tough, so go slow and work in small bites.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

OPENING IT UP

Cut the lid at an angle, slanting inward, so the lid sits on a ledge and can't fall in. Cut a little notch in the lid so it always goes back on the same way.

Some carvers cut the hole in the back or the bottom instead. A bottom hole lets you set the pumpkin right over the light.

Scrape out the seeds and stringy pulp. Then scrape the inside of the front wall until it's about as thick as your thumb, or a crow's toe. A thinner wall is easier to cut and glows brighter.

SAVE THE SEEDS. Rinse them, pull off the strings, let them dry, and toast them. Pumpkin seeds are one of the best snacks a crow could ask for.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

DESIGNS

The classic face: two triangle eyes, a triangle nose, and a wide mouth with square teeth. It's simple and it looks good from far away.

Simple shapes carve best. Circles, triangles and crescents hold together. Thin bridges between cut-out holes break easily.

Draw or poke the design on before you cut. Start with the small pieces in the middle and work outward, so the pumpkin stays strong while you work.

Other ideas: a cat with its back arched, a bat with spread wings, a full moon with a bird flying across it, a skull, a spider web, a ghost. A crow silhouette on a pumpkin is a Halloween favorite.

SHADING: instead of cutting all the way through, peel off only the orange skin and leave the pale flesh underneath. Light glows softly through the thin spots. You can make whole pictures this way, with bright parts, dim parts and dark parts.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

LIGHTING IT

A candle is the old way. Put the lid back on with a little chimney hole cut in it, or the lid will cook. Never leave a candle burning unwatched, and keep it away from dry leaves, feathers and anything else that could catch.

A battery light or a small flashlight is safer and lasts all night.

A jack-o'-lantern looks best after dark, when the light shows through every cut. The face seems to flicker and move.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

MAKING IT LAST

A carved pumpkin starts drying out and going soft right away. Most last three to five days, less in the sun or in warm weather.

Rubbing the cut edges with a little oil slows the drying. Keeping it in the cold at night helps.

When it finally caves in, it's food for squirrels, birds, and the worms in the garden. Nothing about a pumpkin goes to waste.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

A LAST WORD

Every carved pumpkin is different, and every one is a little lopsided. That's the fun of it. Carve the face that's in your head, set it out where people can see it, and light it up when the sun goes down.
