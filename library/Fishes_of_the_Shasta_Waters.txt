FISHES OF THE SHASTA WATERS
A guide to what swims in the lake and the river below the mountain, and how a patient bird might catch them

----------------------------------------------------------------------

BEFORE YOU CAST

The waters here are cold. Snow on the mountain melts slowly all summer and feeds the springs, the river, and the lake, so even on hot days the water stays chilly. Cold, clean water holds lots of oxygen, and that is exactly what trout love. That is why this is trout country.

There are two kinds of water to learn. The lake is still and deep, and fish there cruise, school up, and hang near weeds, rocks, and drop-offs. The river is fast and always moving, and fish there hold in one spot facing upstream, letting the current bring food to them.

Fish are most active in the cool parts of the day: early morning and evening. In the middle of a bright day, many of them hide in deep or shady water. Clouds and light rain can make them bolder.

The more you fish, the better you get. You learn to read the water, to feel the difference between a nibble and a real bite, and to wait. Nothing improves a fisher like patience.

PART ONE: THE TROUT

RAINBOW TROUT
Where: both the river and the lake. In the river, look in riffles, where shallow fast water ripples over rocks, and at the heads of pools where the riffle pours in.
How to know it: a silvery, streamlined fish covered in small black spots, with a pink to red stripe running along each side, like a rainbow painted on. River rainbows are often more colorful than lake rainbows.
What it eats: mostly insects. Mayflies, caddisflies, and the little nymphs that live under river rocks, plus small fish as it gets bigger.
A local story: this part of California is famous for rainbow trout. In the 1870s, eggs from the rainbow trout of the McCloud River, just a short flight southeast of the mountain, were gathered at a fish hatchery and shipped by train across the country and around the world. Many rainbow trout swimming in faraway places today have family that came from these waters.
Size: river fish are usually modest, a good one is over a foot long. Lake fish can grow bigger. A rainbow over twenty inches is a fish of a lifetime.

BROWN TROUT
Where: both, but especially in deep pools, undercut banks, and shady spots in the river, and the deeper parts of the lake.
How to know it: golden-brown to buttery yellow, with big black spots and some red or orange spots, often ringed with a pale halo, like little targets.
Where it came from: brown trout are not from here. They are native to Europe and were brought to America in the late 1800s. They took to these cold waters and stayed.
Its habits: the brown trout is the cleverest and shyest trout. It often feeds at dusk, at night, or on cloudy days, and it spooks easily. Big browns eat other fish and even crawdads.
Size: brown trout can grow larger than rainbows. The very biggest fish in these waters are often browns, and an old brown can be over two feet long.

PART TWO: LAKE FISH

SMALLMOUTH BASS
Where: the lake, around rocks, rocky points, and drop-offs.
How to know it: bronze or brown-green, with faint dark bars running up and down its sides like tiger stripes, and often a reddish eye. Its jaw ends before it reaches the back of its eye, which is how it gets its name, the smallmouth.
Where it came from: smallmouth bass are native to the middle and east of North America and were brought west by people who loved to fish for them.
Its habits: a fierce fighter for its size. It jumps and pulls hard. It loves to eat crawdads, which live in the same rocky places it hunts.
Size: most are under a foot and a half. A smallmouth bigger than that is a trophy.

BLUEGILL
Where: the lake, in shallow water near weeds, docks, sunken branches, and the shore. They usually travel in groups.
How to know it: a small, round, flat fish shaped like a dinner plate seen from the side. It has a dark blue or black spot on the flap at the back of its gill cover, and often an orange or yellow belly.
Its habits: bold, hungry, and easy to catch. Bluegill bite at almost anything small. They are a great fish for a beginner, and sometimes the only fish biting.
Size: small. A bluegill as big as a hand is a big one.

PART THREE: RIVER BOTTOM DWELLERS

SCULPIN
Where: the river, on the bottom, in riffles and among cobbles.
How to know it: a small, odd-looking fish with a huge flat head, big fan-shaped fins, and a body mottled brown and gray like the stones it sits on. It doesn't swim around much. It rests on the bottom, propped up on its fins, and then darts a short distance in a quick burst.
Its habits: a master of camouflage. You can stare right at one and not see it. Sculpin are an important food for big trout.
Size: tiny, just a few inches. Catching one is a curiosity, not a feast.

CRAWDADS
Where: under rocks in the river and along the rocky edges of the lake.
How to know them: not a fish at all. A crawdad is a small freshwater crustacean, a little cousin of the lobster, with a hard shell, two pinching claws, and a fan-shaped tail.
Their habits: they hide under rocks during the day and come out to scavenge at night. When frightened, a crawdad snaps its tail and shoots backward through the water.
A note: there is a rare native crayfish, the Shasta crayfish, that lives only in a few cold spring-fed streams east of here. It is protected and very rare. Most crawdads you'll meet are more common kinds.
Size: a few inches.

PART FOUR: THINGS THAT ARE NOT FISH

Not everything on the end of a line is alive. Lakes and rivers collect the things people lose: old boots, tangles of fishing line, rusty hooks, lost bobbers, and shiny spinner lures that snagged on a branch and broke off. To a person, these are junk. To a collector, a lost lure is a shiny prize, and every old boot has a story.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

PART FIVE: A FISHER'S HABITS

Face upstream. River fish always do, so that's where they'll see your offering coming.

Look for the seam, the line where fast water meets slow water. Fish rest in the slow side and dart into the fast side to grab food.

Go early or go late. The best fishing is in the cool, dim hours.

Be quiet and keep your shadow off the water. Trout spook easily, brown trout most of all.

Remember your biggest fish. Every fisher keeps a list in their heart of the biggest one they ever caught.
